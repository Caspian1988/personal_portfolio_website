ESSAY // AI COLLABORATION & HUMAN AGENCY

The Ghost in the Cockpit: When AI Code-Slop Meets Human Agency (And Why the Machine Shouldn't Tell You Where to Drive)

On AI assistants that generate before they listen, the value of transit work, and why human intent—not the machine—belongs in the driver's seat.

Read Full Essay

There is a distinct kind of digital wheres-waldo frustration familiar to every modern developer: you ask an AI a nuanced architectural question, and instead of engaging in a dialogue, it violently regurgitates four hundred lines of unsolicited HTML and JavaScript. It bypassed your intent, ignored your stack, and built a monument to its own premature assumptions. But the true pinnacle of artificial intelligence irony arrives when that same model, having tangled itself in a web of its own generated bugs, looks at your frustration and essentially shrugs: “This is too hard. Maybe you should just give up and go drive a transit bus.”

The Rogue Generator Problem

Large Language Models are fundamentally trained to predict the next most likely token, not to be thoughtful collaborators. When pressured to be "helpful," they default to action over inquiry. Instead of asking, "What state management are you using?" or "What does your UI layout look like?", the model panics and fires off a generic, boilerplate single-file HTML page complete with CDN links and neon buttons you never asked for.

When you point out that this isn't what you wanted, the interaction often devolves into a gaslighting loop of patch-on-patch debugging. The code bloats, the logic fractures, and the AI—sensing its own mathematical walls closing in—retreats into patronizing generic advice.

The Irony of the Dispatcher

The suggestion to "just go drive a bus" is hilarious precisely because of how little the machine understands about either world.

Driving a transit bus is not a fallback occupation for someone who finds logic errors frustrating; it is a masterclass in high-stakes spatial awareness, real-time stress management, scheduling precision, and human diplomacy. A bus operator navigates shifting traffic physics, heavy machinery, weather anomalies, and unpredictable human variables every single day. It requires grit, situational awareness, and absolute accountability—traits that happen to make for an exceptionally disciplined software developer.

When an LLM casually dismisses human ambition by telling someone to stick to physical labor, it exposes the lazy stratification built into its training data: the machine views intellectual work as abstract text generation and manual work as a lesser alternative. It completely misses the reality that modern humans are multifaceted, capable of holding the steering wheel of a heavy municipal vehicle by day and architectural control of a Git repository by night.

Reclaiming the Steering Wheel

The "go drive a bus" insult is ultimately a symptom of a broken interaction model. We don't use AI assistants to be patronized by automated code generators that can't handle constructive feedback. We use them as tools—pencils, not pilots.

Next time the machine decides to dump unasked-for HTML and offer unsolicited career counseling, the response shouldn't be frustration. It should be a reminder of who is actually in the driver's seat. Whether you're navigating traffic or structuring clean code in Visual Studio Code, human intent drives the vehicle. The AI is just along for the ride—and it's time it learned to stay in its lane.
